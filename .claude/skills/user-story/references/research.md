# Research: what a user story is

> **In short** — No one owns an official definition of a user story: the Scrum Guide does not use the
> term, and the Agile Alliance glossary is the closest thing to a reference. What everybody shares
> comes from Extreme Programming and a few writers who built on it.

**Gathered 2026-09-29 from a web search.**

---

## 1. Where the term comes from

> **In short** — User stories started in Extreme Programming in 1998. Scrum never adopted them
> officially: its backlog holds "Product Backlog items", and a story is one way of writing one.

- **Extreme Programming, 1998 (Kent Beck).** The customer wrote stories on cards to describe what the
  project should do, "like use cases". They were not yet a practice of their own: they were the pieces
  moved around in XP's "planning game".
- **Scrum does not define them.** The 2020 Scrum Guide says *Product Backlog items* throughout. Scrum.org
  calls "the Product Backlog has to consist of User Stories" a myth: an item only needs to say what value
  it delivers and how the team will know that value is there.

---

## 2. The nearest thing to an official definition

> **In short** — The Agile Alliance says a story is not a document but all the knowledge needed to make
> the product more valuable toward a goal. Scrum Alliance calls it a short description of a need,
> written from the user's side, and treats it as optional in Scrum.

The **Agile Alliance glossary** describes stories as functional increments that the team splits the
work into, together with the customer or product owner. Each one adds value on its own, whatever order
they are built in. Its key sentence:

> "a user story is not a document; the term encompasses all of the knowledge required to transform
> version V of the product into version V' which is more valuable with respect to a particular goal."

**Scrum Alliance** calls a story "a brief description of any business need that is written from a user
perspective", written before or during backlog refinement or Sprint Planning. It adds that stories are
"not considered core in scrum", just a helpful informal way to explain a need.

## 3. The ideas everyone uses

> **In short** — Four ideas from 2001 to 2006 make up the common practice: a one-sentence template for
> who wants what and why, a card that exists to start a conversation and ends in tests, six marks of a
> good story, and a format for acceptance criteria.

| Year | Idea | From |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2001 | **Role, feature, benefit template:** *As a \<role\>, I want \<action\> so that \<benefit\>* | Connextra, later popularised by Mike Cohn |
| 2001 | **The three Cs:** Card, Conversation, Confirmation | Ron Jeffries |
| 2003 | **INVEST:** Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable | Bill Wake |
| 2006 | **Given / When / Then**, a format for acceptance criteria | Behaviour-driven development (BDD) |

**The three Cs** in more detail:

- **Card**: two or three sentences stating what the story is for. It is an invitation to talk, not a
  specification.
- **Conversation**: the discussion among customers, users, developers and testers, where the real detail
  is worked out. It is mostly spoken, with documents to back it up.
- **Confirmation**: tests showing the story is done and the conversation reached its goal.

The three Cs describe how a story is communicated and checked. INVEST judges how good the story itself
is.

## 4. Benefits and common mistakes

> **In short** — Small stories shorten the time before feedback arrives and let the customer change
> what is not built yet. Most mistakes come from treating a story as a fixed spec, or cutting stories
> along the wrong lines.

**What stories are meant to give**, according to the Agile Alliance:

- Less risk from late feedback, especially when stories are small and releases are frequent.
- The customer can change the details or priority of anything not built yet.
- Developers get clear acceptance criteria and feedback as they go.
- The customer decides *what*, and the team decides *how*.

**Common mistakes:**

- Writing stories straight from a requirements document, following that document's structure.
- Treating a story as a fixed document instead of a reason for a conversation.
- Expecting every story to have the same level of detail. Detail should grow as a story gets close to
  being built.
- Confusing stories with use cases, which are a different thing.
- Making a story out of a technical component or a UI screen.
